Self-evolving artificial intelligence virtual cells: A closed-loop system 
from multi-scale modeling to biomedical applications

Abstract
Artificial intelligence virtual cell (AIVC) technology is profoundly reshaping the paradigm of biomedical research. Its core lies 
in the precise simulation of complex biological processes, ranging from molecular interactions to the emergent properties of 
multicellular systems, by integrating the fundamental logic of life sciences. From the predictable flux of gene expression and 
the information processing logic of signaling networks to the programmed memory mechanisms governing cell fate decisions, 
AIVC aims to capture the critical rules of cellular behavior. Notably, with the introduction of generative biomedical agents, 
AIVC is evolving from a passive data-fitting tool into a form of digital life possessing cognitive capabilities. In terms of data 
integration, AIVC deeply fuses multi-modal data, including single-cell transcriptomics, epigenomics, spatial transcriptomics, and 
systematic perturbation data, to construct a comprehensive digital panorama of the cell. At the level of model construction, the 
incorporation of agents has endowed AIVC with unprecedented autonomy. By leveraging the logical reasoning capabilities of 
large language models, AIVC is no longer confined to static causal network inference. Instead, acting like a virtual scientist, it can 
autonomously formulate hypotheses, design virtual perturbation experiments, and achieve model self-iteration and optimization 
through feedback loops. This transition from soft validation to autonomous discovery could render the simulation of cellular 
state transitions under various complex environments more precise and interpretable. Regarding application expansion, AIVC is 
driving a paradigm shift across drug discovery, disease mechanism research, and precision medicine. It not only efficiently identi­
fies potential drugs through virtual screening, but also utilizes the dynamic evolutionary capabilities driven by agents to construct 
programmable and self-correcting virtual human bodies. This, in turn, optimizes clinical trial designs and provides decision 
support for personalized treatment. Despite the promising prospects of AIVC technology, it currently faces challenges regarding 
multi-source data integration, model interpretability, and the translation from in silico simulations to clinical applications. Future 
development should focus on building an interdisciplinary collaborative ecosystem, establishing standardized evaluation bench­
marks, and prioritizing the development of agent models capable of autonomous learning and adaptive evolution. Ultimately, we 
aim to achieve a fundamental transformation from static simulation to intelligent prediction, establishing digital twin systems 
that can accurately map individual health states and autonomously explore disease progression, thereby making a significant 
contribution to the future of personalized medicine.
Keywords: Artificial intelligence; Virtual cell; Multiscale modeling; Digital twin; Drug discovery; Precision medicine

Introduction
Artificial intelligence virtual cell (AIVC) technology is 
reshaping the landscape of biomedical research at an 
unprecedented pace, heralding a profound paradigm 
shift in the life sciences. For a long time, biologists have 
harbored the grand vision of reconstructing cells in silico 
to predict their behavior accurately.[1] This concept dates 
back to the late 1990s, when researchers attempted to 
construct whole-cell computational models—such as 
the development of the VCell platform[2] and the subse­
quent simulation of a Mycoplasma genitalium whole-cell 
model[3]—aimed at understanding the fundamental units 
of life. However, constrained by data scarcity, model 
oversimplification, and limited computational power 
at the time, these early explorations failed to achieve 
widespread breakthroughs,[4] causing the development 
of comprehensive, realistic AIVC models to be regarded

as a “Grand Challenge of 21st Century Life Science”.[5,6] 
Today, AIVC technology is rapidly transforming biomedi­
cal research, signaling a deep transition in the life sciences 
from a traditional “hypothesis-driven” approach to a 
“simulation-first” paradigm.[7,8]
Entering the 2010s, the rapid advancement of single-cell 
omics, spatial transcriptomics, and high-throughput sequenc­
ing has generated massive amounts of experimental data, 
providing a solid foundation for data-driven biological 
modeling. Concurrently, the rise of artificial intelligence 
(AI)—particularly deep learning, generative models, and 
advanced algorithms like transformer networks—has 
made it possible to train complex models that approxi­
mate cellular behavior. For instance, models such as scGPT 
have applied large language model (LLM) concepts to sin­
gle-cell data analysis,[9] enabling the model to “read” gene 
expression patterns and make predictions. Furthermore, 
hybrid transformer-graph neural network (GNN) archi­
tectures integrate cross-modal data to achieve universal 
representations (URs), while neural ordinary differential 
equations (ODEs) are used to accurately simulate the 
complex nonlinear dynamics within cells.[10] These tech­
nological advancements have reignited the early dream 
of the AIVC, propelling AIVC technology into a frontier 
interdisciplinary field connecting molecular biology, cell 
biology, and computational science.[11]
The core objective of AIVC is to integrate the underlying 
logic of life sciences to accurately simulate complex bio­
logical processes, ranging from molecular interactions to 
emergent properties of multicellular systems. This requires 
models not only to capture the predictable flux of gene 
expression but also to decipher the information processing 
logic of signaling networks, the temporal engines of the cell 
cycle, and the programming memory mechanisms of dif­
ferentiation and development.[12] In this regard, the “3+1” 
framework was proposed to emphasize the importance 
of combining three data pillars—prior knowledge, static 
structure, and dynamic state—with a closed-loop active 
learning system, aiming to construct a “cell metaverse” 
capable of active exploration and evolutionary growth.[13] 
Such in-depth understanding and simulation of cellular 
behavior is hailed as the “Turing test” of cell biology; its 
success would significantly accelerate our comprehension 
of life processes and drug discovery. At a more macroscopic 
systems level, agent-based modeling (ABM) provides 
powerful tools for the dynamic simulation of multicellular 
systems,[14] while generative frameworks based on diffu­
sion models, such as Squidiff,[15] have advanced cellular 
dynamic prediction into the era of continuous generation, 
enabling the simulation of cellular evolution across the 
temporal dimension.[16]
In terms of model construction, AIVC is evolving from 
initial single-molecule predictions to dynamic causal 
network inference, leveraging deep learning and genera­
tive frameworks to simulate state transitions and fate 
decisions under various perturbations. Unlike protein 
folding prediction (e.g., AlphaFold), cellular behavior 
is a dynamic evolutionary process of an open system, 
continuously influenced by internal signals and the exter­
nal environment, exhibiting significant spatiotemporal 
variability.[17–19] Therefore, AIVC models need to not 
only accurately predict the steady-state endpoint after 
perturbation but also capture the trajectory of cellular 
state changes over time. For instance, the STATE model 
uses a transformer architecture to model cell populations 
simultaneously, capturing heterogeneity and significantly 
improving perturbation effect prediction. Technologies 
such as clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic 
repeats (CRISPR) provide a unified language for gene per­
turbation experiments, enhancing the model’s ability to 
handle complex genetic interventions.[20] At the molecular 
level, RNA foundation models such as CodonFM are 
dedicated to revealing the complex syntax of codon usage, 
further enriching the underlying rule base of AIVC.[21] 
These models strive to explicitly inject biological prior 
knowledge into computational frameworks—for example, 
by using differential equation models to guide design or 
using GNNs to model gene networks—thereby enhancing 
model interpretability and biological plausibility.[22]
Regarding data integration, the construction of AIVC 
requires the fusion of multimodal data to describe cellular 
molecular characteristics and environmental interactions 
comprehensively. This includes, but is not limited to, 
high-throughput data from single-cell transcriptomics, 
epigenomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and spatial tran­
scriptomics.[23,24] To support this ambitious goal, multiple 
large-scale databases have been established globally. For 
example, the AIVC Atlas built by the Arc Institute (CA, 
USA) aggregates the world’s largest single-cell transcrip­
tomics data, including the scBaseCount database and the 
Tahoe-100M drug perturbation dataset, providing unprec­
edented “fuel” for training general-purpose models.[25] 
Concurrently, the Connectivity Map (CMap) project at the 
Broad Institute ( MA, USA) has accumulated over 1.5 mil­
lion gene expression profiles covering thousands of small 
molecule compounds and genetic interventions, serving as 
an ideal training corpus for AIVC models.[26] Furthermore, 
the release of large-scale datasets such as X-Atlas/Orion[19] 
and the AIVC Pharmacology Initiative (VCPI) launched 
by Ginkgo Bioworks (https://datapoints.ginkgo.bio/updates/
vcpi-blogpost), combined with genetic information revealed 
by population genetics studies such as gsMap,[27] can input 
existing biological knowledge into models, significantly 
enhancing their mechanistic accuracy and cross-individual 
generalization capabilities.[28,29]
The expansion of AIVC applications is driving a para­
digm shift in drug discovery, disease mechanism research, 
and precision medicine.[30] High-throughput screening 
(HTS) and simulation within AIVC can drastically reduce 
experimental costs and accelerate the drug development 
process.[31–33] For example, the DrugReflector framework, 
utilizing omics-driven reinforcement learning, enables 
scalable and optimizable compound identification, sig­
nificantly compressing development cycles.[34] Virtual 
models can generate “virtual perturbation signatures” for 
virtual drug repurposing or mechanism of action (MoA) 
discovery, and provide predictive differential expression 
data for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). In the 
realm of personalized medicine, AIVC holds the promise 
of creating “personal virtual cells” using a patient’s own 
cellular data. The OCTO-VirtualCell (OCTO-vc) model,

Despite the promising prospects of AIVC technology, its 
development faces numerous challenges. These include 
the effective integration of multi-source heterogeneous 
data, the generalization ability of models across cell types 
and complex perturbation scenarios, the interpretability 
of internal model mechanisms (the “black box” problem), 
and the translation from in silico simulation to clinical 
application.[38] The complexity of cellular systems, the 
explosion of data dimensions, and implicit issues such 
as the reproducibility of perturbation responses and 
sparse responses in population heterogeneity pose severe 
tests to the robustness and accuracy of AIVC models.[39] 
Moreover, the long-standing lack of unified evaluation 
benchmarks has hindered the field’s development, making 
direct comparison of different research results difficult. 
To address this, the “Virtual Cell Challenge” initiated by 
the Arc Institute aims to catalyze the development of the 
virtual cell field through unified tasks and standards,[40] 
potentially becoming a milestone platform similar to 
CASP (https://predictioncenter.org/) for protein structure 
prediction.
Here, we propose a conceptual framework for a self-evolv­
ing AIVC that iteratively evolves by leveraging feedback 
from downstream applications to refine its underlying 
biological kernels [Figure 1]. This framework is primar­
ily constituted by four core dimensions. First, through 
physical and mathematical modeling of biological laws 
such as the central dogma and cell fate determination, 
the virtualization of multi-scale biological processes is 
achieved, establishing the biological foundation of the 
AIVC. Second, the system integrates multi-modal omics 
data, including single-cell transcriptomics, proteomics, 
and metabolomics, endowing the virtual cell with pano­
ramic data representation capabilities. On this basis, the 
AIVC not only simulates complex scenarios such as the 
tumor microenvironment (TME) and metabolic disorders 
to elucidate disease mechanisms, but also empowers the 
entire drug discovery process, covering virtual screen­
ing, MoA analysis, and preclinical safety assessment. 
Most critically, through a “build–application–feedback” 
closed-loop mechanism, the AIVC utilizes feedback data 
generated from downstream applications to continuously 
optimize parameters, achieving autonomous evolution of 
the intelligent agent.
As a cutting-edge computational paradigm, an agent is 
not merely a passive simulation tool but an autonomous 
system possessing perception, decision-making, and exe­
cution capabilities.[41] In the field of biological computing, 
agents can actively explore unknown biological spaces and 
dynamically adjust strategies in response to environmental 
changes, much like living organisms.[42] Integrating intel­
ligent agents with the development and application of 
AIVC unleashes immense potential for solving complex 
biomedical problems.[43] Rather than relying on static rule 
inputs, agents transform feedback from drug discovery 
and disease analysis into a driving force for evolution 
through the “build–application–feedback” closed-loop 
mechanism. This continuous interactive learning enables 
the autonomous refinement of the underlying biological 
kernel, thereby facilitating a fundamental leap from sim­
ple data fitting to deep biological logical reasoning and 
propelling the advancement of AIVC.
Looking ahead, the future direction of AIVC lies in building 
a cross-disciplinary collaborative ecosystem, establishing 
open and shared standardized datasets and evaluation 
benchmarks, and developing autonomous dynamic intel­
ligent models. For example, the CellForge framework 
proposed by teams from Yale University and Google 
DeepMind operates as a fully autonomous multi-agent 
system (MAS) capable of automating the entire process 
from biological data to optimized computational models, 
foreshadowing the pioneering role of “AI Scientists” 
in biological research.[44] Through continuous iterative 
optimization and “lab-in-the-loop” mechanisms, AIVC is 
poised to achieve a paradigm shift in life science research 
from “static simulation” to “intelligent prediction”. Ulti­
mately, the development of AIVC will be dedicated to 
constructing digital twin virtual humans capable of pre­
cisely simulating individual health states and disease 
progression, contributing significantly to the future of 
personalized medicine and fundamentally transforming the 
way humanity understands and improves life and health.[45,46]
Building AIVC Through Integrated Life Science Fundamentals
Virtualization of multi-scale core biological processes to 
construct an AIVC model
Modeling core elements: Digitalization of fundamental 
biological processes
Deep computational modeling of life’s fundamental unit—
AIVC models—is not technological fantasies, but is 
grounded in profound deconstruction of the intricate and 
ordered biological mechanisms within the cell.[47] The 
intrinsic regularity, hierarchy, and dynamic properties 
of these mechanisms provide an indispensable biological 
blueprint for constructing predictive digital twins.[48] 
From the decoding and execution of genetic information, 
to the integration and response of intra- and extracellular 
signals, to the precise timing of the cell life cycle and the 
directed evolution of multicellular fates, the cell harbors 
rich and structured “biological algorithms” that naturally 
guide the architecture and learning paradigms of virtual 
models.[22]
The hierarchical flow of genetic information forms the 
foundational procedural framework for AIVC modeling.

not static codes; rather, they are selectively transcribed

modular computational pipelines and imposes direct 
constraints on parametric objects (e.g., RNA abundance, 
protein concentration). This enables AI models to effec­

genotype and molecular phenotypes.[51]
Life is far from the static execution of genetic information; 
cells continuously sense and respond to microenvironmen­
tal changes.[52] Highly interconnected signaling networks 
exist within the cell—ligands bind to cell surface receptors, 
triggering cascades that involve reversible covalent modi­
fications such as phosphorylation and ubiquitination, 
engaging numerous molecular switches and amplifiers 
such as G-proteins, kinases, and phosphatases, and ulti­
mately converging on the regulation of the activity of key 
transcription factors. This highly networked structure 
itself resembles the intrinsic topology of deep neural 
networks.[53] Its core function lies in processing multidi­
mensional signal inputs (e.g., growth factors, metabolic 
status, stress) and integrating them nonlinearly (e.g., 
through bistable switches, feedback oscillations) to ulti­
mately drive binary or graded functional outputs (e.g., 
gene transcription switches, metabolic reprogramming, 
cytoskeletal reorganization).[54] The dynamic properties 
of signaling networks—their inherent information pro­
cessing logic, state transition thresholds, robustness to 
perturbation, and potential emergent behaviors—precisely 
provide critical algorithmic prototypes and mathematical 
constraints for building AIVC dynamic models with envi­
ronmental adaptability.
The periodic rhythm of cell proliferation demands that 
virtual models incorporate precise timing logic and state 
transition mechanisms.[55] Driven by developmental 
cues and environmental signals, eukaryotic cells strictly 
progress through sequential phases: pre-DNA synthesis 
(G1), DNA replication (S), mitotic preparation (G2), and 
division execution (M). This progression is governed by a 
molecular engine composed of cyclin-dependent kinases 
(CDKs) and cyclins, whose activity oscillates under the 
control of complex positive and negative feedback loops 
(e.g., inhibitory phosphorylation of CDKs by WEE1 G2 
checkpoint kinase [WEE1]/myelin transcription factor 1 
[MYT1] and activating dephosphorylation by cell divi­
sion cycle 25 [CDC25]) and checkpoint mechanisms (e.g., 
DNA damage arrest at G2/M).[56] Key checkpoints, such 
as the spindle assembly checkpoint, involve highly condi­
tional responses to intracellular biochemical states (e.g., 
halting division if microtubules are unattached), directly 
forming the basis for conditional state-transition-based 
model logic units. Cell cycle models must dynamically 
integrate exogenous signals such as nutrient status, energy 
levels, and growth factor inputs, and execute timer-like 
switch behaviors.[57] This intrinsic logic of periodic oscil­
lation and strict sequence-dependent state transitions 
requires model architectures to possess core capabilities 
for simulating phase transitions, temporal control, and 
checkpoint control.[8,58]
The most challenging aspect of constructing high-fidelity 
AIVC lies in simulating their fate decisions and developmen­
tal trajectories beyond the single cell cycle. In multicellular 
organisms, pluripotent stem cells, guided by specific 
spatiotemporal signal combinations (e.g., concentration 
gradients and temporal patterns of wingless-related inte­
gration site [Wnt], Notch, transforming growth factor-β 
[TGF-β]), undergo lineage specification into stable cell 
types with distinct morphologies, functions, and molecular 
characteristics (e.g., neurons, muscle cells, and epithelial 
cells). This process involves the reprogramming of chro­
matin states (e.g., methylation and acetylation marks) 
and transcription factor networks.[59] These epigenetic 
imprints create stable “overwrites” or “shields” over the 
original developmental program, forming persistent cellu­
lar identity memory. During morphogenesis, cells perform 
spatially operative behaviors—such as directed migration, 
polarization, and local adhesion regulation—based on the 
interpretation of chemical or mechanical signal gradients, 
shaping complex tissue architectures. Understanding and 
quantifying this “cellular memory”, a feature encoded 
by the state of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) and 
dependent on historical instructions (differentiation tra­
jectories), along with the spatial self-organization logic 
of intercellular coordination, is crucial for building AIVC 
models capable of predicting stem cell differentiation 
paths, regenerative medicine intervention outcomes, or 
tissue homeostasis abnormalities. The underlying algo­
rithms of gradient sensing, state memory, and collective 
behavior profoundly inform the design of complex spa­
tiotemporal modeling (e.g., recurrent networks, GNNs, 
MASs).[60]
At its core, the construction of AIVC model represents a 
deep, structured integration of known biological principles 
across scales—from molecules and networks to cellular 
behaviors. The predictable flow of gene expression, the 
information processing logic of signaling networks, the 
timing engine of the cell cycle, and the programming 
memory mechanisms of differentiation and development 
collectively constitute a set of highly organized and logical 
foundational rules. The deconstruction, quantification, 
and formal expression of these rules not only make 
modeling virtual life possible but also provide continuous 
vital biological inspiration and constraint frameworks 
for the design of next-generation AI model architectures. 
The coexistence of profound biological complexity and 
inherent regularity is precisely the cornerstone of AIVC 
modeling engineering.
Expanding model capabilities: From organoid insights 
to the construction of intelligible microenvironments
Organoids—self-organized three-dimensional (3D) mini-
organs from induced or tissue-specific stem cells—reca­
pitulate key structural and functional features of native 
tissues, creating a powerful in vitro platform for disease 
modeling, drug screening, and regenerative research.[61] 
They can mimic TMEs, enabling insight into immu­
no-oncology,[62] and serve as fertile ground for studying 
intercellular interactions in neurology and immunology. 
Compared to cell culture-based in vitro methodologies, 
organoids provide accessible models for the investiga­
tion of processes such as intercellular crosstalk between 
different cell types without the need for complex in vivo

Computational biology and machine learning can model

Current vascularized organoid technology has achieved 
breakthrough advances in simulating early vascular

challenges: first, complex microenvironmental parame­

and immune factor interactions are difficult to synchro­

Second, the emergent responses of multi-level cellular

coupling of signals among endothelial, smooth muscle, 
parenchymal, and immune cells in both development and 
disease; third, high-throughput experimental condition 
screening, involving multi-factor combinatorial optimiza­
tion and genetic editing perturbations, is constrained by 
time-intensive and laborious trial-and-error processes.[65] 
These limitations present pivotal opportunities for AIVC 
technology. The core value of AIVC lies in transcending 
physical model constraints by leveraging explainable algo­
rithms to actively reason and predict emergent behaviors 
arising from the synergistic effects of highly parameterized 
spatial constraints, multi-pathway regulatory nodes, and 
multi-cellular interaction coupling mechanisms. By construct­
ing differentiable biological simulators that integrate 
spatiotemporally resolved cellular behavior prediction 
modules and multi-scale environmental parameter inference 
engines, causal mechanisms within biological systems 
are inversely decoupled. For instance, the vascularized 
cardiac organoid differentiation framework encompassing 
four-stage regulation from mesoderm orderly induction 
to vascular maturation,[66] as well as the multi-fluores­
cence-labeled brain organoid long-term in vivo imaging 
platform and extracellular matrix (ECM) regulatory mechanism 
framework,[67] can be transformed into key data primi­
tives for AIVC training.[66] Future AIVC should overcome 
the confines of static correlation analysis by utilizing genera­
tive modeling to actively design and predict organoid 
differentiation pathways, such as migration thresholds 
for cross-organ vascularization schemes, and establish 
dynamic inference capabilities for pathological microenvi­
ronments (e.g., simulating aberrant signal transduction 
between vascular endothelial and smooth muscle cells in 
diabetic states). This AIVC architecture, integrating physical 
microenvironment logic, multi-pathway synergistic rules, 
and single-cell resolution interaction predictions, will serve 
as a core driver to overcome current in vitro modeling 
bottlenecks and enable a transition from organ structural 
simulation to in-depth physiological/pathological func­
tional prediction. This virtual paradigm will markedly 
advance the paradigm shift in drug toxicity assessment 
from organ structural simulation to pathological function 
prediction.
From static observation to dynamic mechanism: Re­
constructing the biological core of AIVC agents
The construction of AIVC agents centers on the deep 
integration of the fundamental logic of life sciences, aim­
ing to precisely simulate and predict complex biological 
processes ranging from molecular interactions to emer­
gent multicellular phenomena within a computational 
environment. As the fundamental unit of life, the cell 
operates according to precise biophysical and biochemi­
cal laws, exhibiting complexity and dynamics that far 
exceed the scope of static observation. While traditional 
whole-cell models have attempted to reproduce these pro­
cesses using sets of biochemical equations based on first 
principles, they are limited by immense computational 
demands, strict parameter dependencies, and an inability 
to adequately characterize the inherent stochasticity and 
heterogeneity of eukaryotic cells as open systems.[44] 
Consequently, they struggle to comprehensively predict 
systemic consequences such as gene silencing. Therefore, 
current AIVC models are dedicated to spontaneously 
learning these underlying rules from massive multi-scale 
biological datasets, facilitating a fundamental shift from 
identifying simple statistical associations to revealing 
dynamic causal networks.[68]
In constructing the underlying logic at the molecular level, 
AIVC models strive to capture the non-linear interactions 
among the genome, transcriptome, and proteome. The 
internal “language” of the cell is essentially a complex 
array of chemical and physical signals that drive the regu­
lation of tens of thousands of genes. For instance, the 
breakthrough progress of AlphaFold in protein structure 
prediction has demonstrated the potential of deep learning 
to resolve the physical properties of biological macromole­
cules.[69] Furthermore, models such as CodonFM delve 
deeper into the complex syntax of codon usage, revealing 
subtle mechanisms of gene expression regulation.[21] 
These models can even predict the specific impacts of 
synonymous mutations on mRNA translation efficiency 
and protein abundance, providing precise guidance for 
gene editing. This approach, which utilizes geometric neu­
ral networks and biological language models to parse the 
probabilities of intermolecular interactions, establishes a 
solid microscopic foundation for understanding macro­
scopic cellular behaviors.
Proceeding to the logical construction at the cellular and 
multicellular levels, models focus on cell differentiation, 
cell cycle, and dynamic responses to environmental per­

Multimodal omics data integration for AIVC reconstruction
Application of genomic data in construction and analy­
sis of in silico cell models
Whole-genome sequencing technologies (including short-
read and long-read sequencing) enable high-throughput 
analysis of an organism’s entire genome, providing powerful 
tools for genetic research and clinical translation. These 
techniques are widely applied to identify single-nucleo­
tide polymorphisms (SNPs) and structural variants (SVs), 
which significantly impact gene function, expression, and 
disease pathogenesis.[74] Genomic data, such as genetic 
variation information, are integrated into in silico cell 
models to elucidate cellular behaviors, disease mecha­
nisms, predict therapeutic responses, and reveal cell–cell 
interactions and signaling pathways, thereby establishing 
a theoretical foundation for optimizing treatment regi­
mens and developing novel strategies.[75]
RNA-seq technology delivers highly sensitive, high-reso­
lution transcriptome-wide expression profiles and serves 
as a core tool for analyzing gene expression dynamics. 
Single-cell transcriptomics (scRNA-seq) resolves cellular 
heterogeneity, uncovering rare cell types, specific sub­
populations, and their roles in TMEs and developmental 
processes.[76] Transcriptomic data are utilized not only for 
expression analysis but also for constructing and simu­
lating GRNs using methods such as Bayesian networks, 
revealing gene interactions and key regulatory factors.[77] 
Integration of these data into in silico cell models facilitates 
a systems-level understanding of how GRNs coordinate 
to respond to diverse conditions.[78]
DNA methylation and histone modifications (e.g., acetylation 
and lactylation) constitute critical epigenetic mechanisms 
regulating gene expression. Aberrations in these modi­
fications are closely associated with diseases, including 
tumor suppressor gene silencing in cancer, as well as with 
abnormal cellular differentiation processes. Technologies 
such as chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing 
(ChIP-seq) are extensively used to analyze transcription 
factor-target gene interactions and their roles in disease 
pathogenesis. Understanding epigenetic modification patterns, 
particularly during stem cell differentiation, is essential 
for elucidating cell fate determination mechanisms.[79] 
This epigenetic regulatory information is fundamental for 
accurately simulating cellular states and differentiation 
trajectories within in silico cell models.
At the level of cellular microenvironment reconstruction, 
advances in spatial omics technologies enable virtual cells 
to incorporate spatiotemporal dimensions. DiffusionST 
innovatively transforms gene expression data into spatial 
images, leveraging the powerful denoising capabilities of 
diffusion models to successfully address the challenge of 
high noise in spatial transcriptomics data. Its stable spatial 
domain recognition ability, maintained even with up to 
50% missing data, enables virtual cells to accurately simu­
late key biological processes such as immune cell infiltration 
and stromal remodeling in the TME.[80] This image-based 
model for biological data reconstruction provides a novel 
methodology for constructing high-fidelity virtual TMEs. It 
is worth noting that the construction of virtual cells must 
adhere to underlying biological logic. Comparative studies 
in the field of antibody optimization have revealed that 
simple convolutional neural networks (CNNs) informed 
by DNA mutation mechanisms significantly outperform 
large-scale protein language models in predicting affinity 
maturation.[81] This indicates that the development of AIVC 
should prioritize biological mechanism-driven approaches 
over model complexity, especially when simulating pro­
cesses such as somatic hypermutation, where integrating 
underlying biological rules is crucial.

Integration of metabolomics into functional predictions 
of AIVC models
The metabolic network is a complex system of intercon­
nected biochemical reactions within a cell or organism, 
such as carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, and 
amino acid metabolism. It reflects the coordinated flow 
of matter and energy, thereby constituting the foundation 
of cellular metabolism and physiological functions.[39] 
Technological advances in liquid chromatography–mass 
spectrometry (LC–MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance 
(NMR) have significantly enhanced the efficiency and 
accuracy of qualitative and quantitative analysis of small 
molecule metabolites. Metabolomic perturbation analy­
sis, coupled with integrated metabolomic-transcriptomic 
analyses linking metabolites to upstream regulatory mecha­
nisms such as gene expression, facilitates the identification of 
biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Integrating metabolomic 
data with other omics datasets is pivotal for construct­
ing precise AIVC models. These models predict cellular 
metabolic functions and responses under various envi­
ronmental conditions, such as drug treatments, thereby 
aiding drug screening and precision medicine.
As a pivotal link connecting genotype to phenotype, 
metabolomics characterizes the panoramic landscape of 
small-molecule metabolites within biological systems, 
thereby providing core chemical fingerprints for the con­
struction of high-fidelity AIVC models. Since metabolite 
abundance sensitively reflects the real-time physiological 
state of cells under internal and external stimuli, deep 
mining based on large-scale metabolomics data has 
become a prerequisite for achieving digital simulation of 
cellular functions. However, confronted with the high-di­
mensional, sparse, and non-linear massive data generated 
by modern mass spectrometry technologies, traditional 
statistical methods struggle to capture complex non-lin­
ear relationships. This limitation has prompted the 
establishment of a deep synergistic mode between AI and 
metabolomics, serving as a critical computational engine 
for decoding the chemical language of life and recon­
structing virtual cellular metabolic networks.[84]
Recent systematic studies leveraging high-throughput untar­
geted metabolomics have unveiled the cascade effects 
triggered by drug perturbations within cellular meta­
bolic networks, providing a critical data foundation for 
building dynamic AIVC models.[85] Constructing drug–
metabolite–enzyme interaction models via GNNs can 
transcend the limitations of chemical structure similarity, 
reveal hidden pharmacological poly-pharmacology, and 
offer novel perspectives for drug repurposing.
At the foundational data layer of AIVC construction, 
AI algorithms have significantly enhanced the accuracy 
of metabolite qualification and quantification, ensuring 
the reliability of model inputs. Addressing common noise 
and drift issues in mass spectrometry signal analysis, deep 
learning models such as DeepSpectra have effectively 
improved spectral quantitative precision. Meanwhile, 
ChromAlignNet, incorporating CNN, has optimized the 
alignment and identification processes of chromatographic 
peaks. When combined with methods such as SERRF to 
eliminate systematic errors across multi-batch experi­
ments, these approaches provide a standardized material 
basis for AIVC. Furthermore, in the evolution from static 
features to dynamic networks, the introduction of a sys­
tems biology perspective enables AI to simulate complex 
biochemical reaction pathways. GNNs utilize topological 
learning to infer potential associations between metabolites, 
while metabolic flux analysis (MFA) optimized by artifi­
cial neural networks (ANNs) has significantly improved 
the prediction efficiency of intracellular material fluxes. 
Additionally, multi-omics data fusion through frame­
works such as MOSAE overcomes scale discrepancies in 
heterogeneous data, constructing comprehensive models 
that reflect the panoramic state of cells, which is crucial 
for understanding the metabolic reprogramming mecha­
nisms underlying complex diseases.[86,87]

Strategies and paradigms for integrating multimodal 
data in the construction of AIVC
The construction of comprehensive, high-precision AIVC 
models hinges on the deep integration of multi-modal 
data, encompassing transcriptomics, proteomics, and 
metabolomics.[94,95] This integration seeks to transcend 
static snapshots at single molecular levels, using a uni­
fied computational framework to elucidate dynamic 
cellular responses and causal mechanisms at cross-organ, 
cross-species, and temporal dimensions.[44]
Regarding the integration of cross-organ, cross-species, or 
longitudinal datasets, AIVC models predominantly rely 
on the construction of URs and large-scale pre-training 
strategies. To overcome data heterogeneity, the framework 
proposed by Bunne et al[10] utilizes a hybrid transformer-
GNN architecture. Through self-supervised learning, this 
approach maps multi-modal data—including scRNA-seq, 
spatial transcriptomics, and microscopy images—into a 
unified embedding space, effectively bridging cross-scale 
semantic gaps. For instance, the SToFM model uses an 
SE(2) Transformer neural network to extract multi-scale 
information from spatial transcriptomics slides, facilitating 
the expansion from single cells to multicellular tissues.[70] 
In terms of cross-species integration, the TranscriptFormer 
model, trained on 112 million cells across 12 species, 
has demonstrated the capacity to capture evolutionary 
conservation and divergence, thereby enabling knowledge 
transfer.[96] For longitudinal datasets, traditional single-
cell snapshots struggle to capture dynamic processes. 
While methods such as RNA velocity offer inferential 
perspectives,[97] future AIVC models, such as Squidiff, are 
increasingly leveraging generative models (e.g., diffusion 
models) and semantic latent space operations.[15] These 
approaches predict continuous developmental trajectories 
and non-additive gene perturbation effects, enabling the 
simulation of cellular state evolution even in the absence 
of dense temporal measurements. For instance, SATURN, 
as a deep learning method, generates universal cell embed­
dings by integrating protein language model embeddings 
with RNA expression data, enabling effective cross-species 
integration and analysis.[98] Furthermore, the program­
mable virtual human (PVH) framework proposed by Wu 
et al[99] attempts to achieve a genuine span from molecular 
to human scales by integrating physics-informed neural 
networks (PINNs) with microphysiological systems.[99]

inference and disease progression prediction, AIVC models

the model.[13] (4) Deeply embedding biological mecha­
nisms into models, for instance, by constraining the latent 
space with GRNs. This enhances the interpretability of 
transcription factor–target gene pathways and ensures 
that model outputs are biologically plausible.
The deep integration of single-cell multi-omics and AI is 
propelling AIVC models towards a multi-scale integration 
paradigm.[109] The core strategy for constructing high-fi­
delity virtual cells lies in moving beyond single-dimensional 
static snapshots to systematic integration of multi-modal 
data, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, 
and spatial imaging.[110] The application of high-perfor­
mance large models has demonstrated that integrating 
PPINs with subcellular localization information is crucial 
for enhancing the biological realism of these models.[93] 
This, for example, not only aids in identifying key sign­
aling pathways driving disease progression and immune 
evasion mechanisms in oncology research, but also helps 
elucidate complex interaction patterns, such as neuron–
glia interactions in neuroscience research.
Concurrently, technological breakthroughs, exemplified 
by platforms such as Perturb-Multi, have achieved precise 
mapping of genotypes to multi-modal phenotypes by 
combining CRISPR perturbations with spatial transcrip­
tomics and morphological imaging. This provides a solid 
foundation for deciphering cellular plasticity and dynamic 
responses under pathological conditions.[111]
From a computational perspective, there is an urgent need 
to focus on developing end-to-end integration strategies, 
such as self-supervised machine learning. By leveraging 
generative adversarial networks (GANs) or GNNs, chal­
lenges in cross-modal data alignment and spatio-temporal 
dynamic modeling can be overcome, ultimately extracting 
low-dimensional cellular component representations 
from high-dimensional heterogeneous data.[112]
Although challenges remain, such as improving spatial reso­
lution, establishing specific databases, and standardizing 
workflows, by constructing panoramic and generalizable 
representations, AIVC will gain the capability to simulate 
cell state evolution under differentiation and drug pertur­
bations. This will significantly reduce experimental costs 
and provide a novel predictive paradigm for biomarker 
discovery, drug target screening, and precision medicine 
applications.

Application of AIVC in disease mechanism research
AIVC models in cancer research
Cancer, as one of the major global health threats, possesses 
intricate biological properties that often render traditional 
experimental models inadequate for fully recapitulating 
the complexity of the TME. The emergence of AIVC models 
offers new perspectives and tools for cancer research. 
With the rapid advancement of biomedical technologies, 
the application of virtual models in cancer research has 
become increasingly widespread, providing scientists with 
novel tools to gain deeper insights into the complexity of 
the TME, multicellular interactions, and metabolic repro­
gramming in cancer cells. By constructing virtual TMEs, 
researchers can simulate interactions between tumor cells 
and surrounding cells and stroma, thereby revealing the 
biological characteristics of tumors and their metastatic 
mechanisms.[113]
The TME refers to the complex network comprising 
tumor cells and their surrounding non-tumor cells (such 
as immune cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells), ECM, 
and secreted factors. Through intercellular interactions 
and secreted signaling molecules, the TME regulates 
tumor growth, metastasis, and response to therapy.[114]

Cancer cells exhibit significant metabolic reprogramming 
during growth and dissemination, enabling survival under

process not only supports rapid adenosine triphosphate 
(ATP) production but also promotes the generation of 
intermediate metabolites required for biosynthesis. AIVC 
models play a crucial role in analyzing cancer cell metabolic 
pathways. Through computer-aided drug design (CADD) 
and virtual screening, researchers can simulate dynamic 
changes in different metabolic pathways and predict the 
efficacy of specific metabolic inhibitors. Structure-based 
virtual screening methods can rapidly identify inhibitors 
targeting key metabolic enzymes, such as pyruvate kinase 
M2 (PKM2) and glutaminase. Inhibitors targeting these 
enzymes demonstrate promising antitumor activity in 
preclinical models.[119,120] Virtual screening for metabolic 
inhibitors provides novel avenues for cancer therapy. By 
combining computational simulations with experimental 
validation, researchers can screen for metabolic inhibitors 
with potential therapeutic efficacy. This virtual screen­
ing-based strategy not only accelerates drug discovery but 
also offers new possibilities for personalized treatment.
Cancer metastasis involves multiple biological mecha­
nisms, including cell invasiveness, migratory capacity, 
and microenvironmental adaptation. Cancer cells acquire 
enhanced migratory ability through epithelial–mesenchy­
mal transition (EMT). Intercellular interactions within the 
TME promote cancer cell growth and metastasis. Cancer 
cells establish a favorable microenvironment for growth 
by utilizing mechanisms such as angiogenesis and matrix 
remodeling.[121] AIVC models hold significant value in 
predicting cancer metastasis. By developing computational 
models, researchers can simulate the metastatic process of 
cancer cells in vivo and predict the likelihood and kinetics 
of metastasis. GANs show considerable promise in pre­
dicting metastatic patterns. Through adversarial training 
of generator and discriminator networks, GANs can gen­
erate high-quality synthetic data to train other machine 
learning models. In cancer research, this can be used to 
generate virtual patient data, enriching training datasets 
and enhancing model generalizability.
With the rapid development of computer science and 
biomedical technologies, the application of AIVC mod­
els in cancer research continues to expand.[122] Progress 
in AI, machine learning, and big data analytics enables 
AIVC models to simulate the TME and cellular behavior 
with increasing precision. The introduction of digital twin 
technology allows researchers to create virtual replicas of 
patients and update physiological parameters in realtime, 
facilitating personalized treatment strategies.[48] Inter­
disciplinary collaboration is a key driver for advancing 
AIVC model research. Cancer research spans multiple 
fields, including biology, medicine, computer science, 
and engineering. Effective integration of knowledge and 
technologies from these diverse domains necessitates 
interdisciplinary cooperation. AIVC models demonstrate 
immense potential in personalized medicine. By creating 
patient-specific AIVC models, clinicians can simulate various 
treatment scenarios prior to intervention, enabling the 
selection of optimal therapeutic regimens.[123]

Research on metabolic diseases faces complex biological

metabolomics, and computational biology, researchers can

metabolic diseases such as diabetes. Its core lies in inte­

machine learning algorithms, forming a complementary 
hybrid modeling strategy that effectively enhances the

this technology’s potential to improve clinical outcomes,

Applications of AIVC in drug discovery and development
From virtual screening to AIVC: Spearheading new 
breakthroughs in drug discovery
The application of AIVC offers a novel perspective and 
methodology for drug research and development (R&D), 
particularly in drug screening and preclinical studies. Tra­
ditional drug development is plagued by high R&D costs, 
protracted development cycles, and high failure rates.[141] 
In this context, the introduction of AIVC presents new 
opportunities to effectively enhance drug discovery efficiency, 
reduce costs, and accelerate the transition to clinical 
stages. The potential of AI technology in drug discovery is 
manifested in several key aspects. First, AI can analyze vast 
amounts of biological data efficiently to identify potential 
drug targets and candidate compounds, thereby increasing 
the success rate of drug screening. Second, AIVC enables 
virtual screening to rapidly assess the biological activity 
and safety of compounds, reducing reliance on animal 
experiments and lowering R&D costs.[31] Furthermore, 
deep learning algorithms can optimize the drug design 
process by predicting drug–target interactions, enhancing 
efficacy and safety. Beyond efficiency, AIVC facilitates the 
advancement of precision medicine. Through personalized 
drug screening and optimization, these tools assist researchers 
in better understanding disease mechanisms, leading to the 
development of more targeted therapeutic strategies.[142]
Virtual screening is a computer-aided drug discovery 
method that rapidly identifies potential drug candidates 
by simulating the interactions between molecules and 
targets.[143] Utilizing computer algorithms to analyze 
molecular structures and properties, this technology can 
identify biologically active molecules within massive com­
pound libraries.[144] In recent years, driven by increased 
computational power and algorithmic advancements, 
virtual screening has become an essential tool in drug 
discovery.[145] It not only reduces experimental costs and 
time but also improves screening efficiency and accuracy. 
For instance, deep learning-based virtual screening methods 
can automatically extract molecular features, thereby 
enhancing success rates and accuracy. HTS is a method 
capable of rapidly assessing and validating the biological 
activity of large numbers of compounds, typically combining 
automated equipment with multiplex detection technolo­
gies. Its primary advantage lies in the ability to process 
thousands of samples in a short period, rapidly identifying 
active compounds. However, HTS has limitations; it often 
relies on simplified biological models, which may lead to 
the oversight of complex biological responses.[146]
Virtual screening technology is currently undergoing a para­
digm shift driven by AIVC technology.[143] While traditional 
virtual screening primarily focuses on interactions between 
molecules and single targets, AIVC platforms demonstrate 
robust processing capabilities, enabling the simultaneous 
and efficient screening of millions of compounds.[147] Cru­
cially, the integration of AIVC technology with generative AI 
has enabled “generative virtual screening”. This approach 
not only evaluates existing compound libraries but also 
designs and assesses novel compound structures in silico 
that do not exist in nature. This methodology significantly 
expands the explorable chemical space and markedly 
increases the probability of discovering lead compounds 
with novel scaffolds.[148]
A further breakthrough of AIVC lies in its ability to 
conduct virtual screening at the level of complex disease 
phenotypes. Unlike traditional single-target screening, 
modern AIVC systems can perform phenotypic screening 
based on an understanding of the multifactorial, net­
worked nature of diseases.[48] This demonstrates immense 
potential for drug discovery in complex polygenic diseases 
such as Alzheimer’s disease or diabetes. By simulating 
entire pathophysiological processes rather than isolated 
targets, AIVC is expected to identify compounds that 
act on more critical nodes and possess higher potential 
for clinical success. Another significant advancement of 
AIVC in virtual screening is the enhancement of dynamic 
screening capabilities.[149] Traditional virtual screening is 
typically static, whereas AIVC can simulate the dynamic 
processes of compound–target binding, the induction of 
downstream signals, and subsequent cellular responses. 
This dynamic perspective is particularly valuable for 
evaluating novel drug modalities, such as allosteric 
modulators or molecular glues, which require structural 
changes to function. Additionally, AIVC can simulate 
adaptive resistance mechanisms that may arise during 
long-term drug administration, aiding in the early identi­
fication of candidate compounds less likely to induce drug 
resistance.[150]
Currently, the widespread application of AIVC tech­
nology faces challenges regarding data quality, model 
interpretability, and regulatory approval. Addressing these 
challenges requires close interdisciplinary collaboration

MoA research and AIVC
AIVC modeling and the study of drug MoA constitute a 
mutually empowering bidirectional system. The construc­
tion of virtual cells integrates drug perturbation omics 
data and knowledge of mechanisms, while the trained 
models can effectively simulate complex drug–cell interac­
tions, providing a reliable computational platform for 
new drug screening and mechanistic exploration.[151]
Understanding the MoA is a critical component of drug 
development. Clarifying the molecular mechanism of 
a drug not only aids in identifying potential targets but 
also allows for the prediction of efficacy and side effects, 
thereby improving development success rates.[152] For 
instance, understanding the regulatory mechanisms of 
anticancer drugs on signaling pathways can guide the 
development of new therapies, reveal resistance mecha­
nisms, and optimize treatment regimens.
AI and virtual cell technologies play a significant role in 
MoA simulation. AI identifies drug–target interactions by 
analyzing vast quantities of biological data and utilizes 
deep learning to automatically recognize cellular phe­
notypic changes following drug treatment.[153] AI-driven 
virtual cell models can simulate the intracellular dynamic 
behavior of drugs, predicting biological responses and 
potential side effects, thus providing a basis for personal­
ized medicine and precision drug development.
AIVC demonstrates the potential for a paradigm shift 
from static description to dynamic simulation. Modern 
AIVC integrates multi-level information, including signal 
transduction, metabolic regulation, and gene expression 
networks, to comprehensively simulate the impact of 
drugs on complex systems. AIVC based on models such 
as VAEs and scGPT can predict transcriptomic responses 
of drugs in novel cellular states, as well as the synergistic 
or antagonistic effects of drug combinations.[107] Another 
breakthrough of AIVC is the integration of spatiotem­
poral data to simulate the dynamic distribution of drugs 
in subcellular compartments and their time-dependent 
effects, providing key insights into the subcellular sites of 
action and kinetics of drugs.[154]
Successful cases include the use of AI to investigate the 
mechanism of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) 
inhibitors in the treatment of non-small cell lung can­
cer (NSCLC). Through molecular dynamics simulations, 
researchers observed drug–target binding and the impact 
on signaling pathways, providing a structural basis for the 
development of next-generation inhibitors, while simul­
taneously identifying new targets and optimizing drug 
structures.[155]
Target identification is a crucial pathway for studying 
the pathogenesis of clinical diseases. The basic workflow 
involves identifying disease-related genes and proteins 
through bioinformatics and genomic analysis; validating 
their biological functions using techniques such as gene 
knockout and overexpression; and assessing the effects of 
small molecule compounds on targets via HTS.[107] AI and 
machine learning technologies have significantly enhanced 
the efficiency and accuracy of target identification.[156]
AIVC in target identification has evolved from data 
analysis to systems biology simulation. By integrating 
multi-omics data and deep learning algorithms, AIVC 
constructs precise cellular system models to identify 
potential targets that are difficult to discover using tradi­
tional methods.[157,158] AIVC can simulate gene expression 
networks and protein interaction dynamics within the 
TME, while simultaneously considering genomic, tran­
scriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data to provide 
comprehensive target assessment. Notably, AIVC can 
simulate the impact of rare mutations on target selection, 
construct patient-specific digital twin models, and design 
new targeted protein structures via generative AI.[159] 
Deep learning technologies can also analyze cellular mor­
phological features to identify disease-specific phenotypes 
and analyze genomic data to identify disease-related 
mutations and signaling pathways.
Future target identification will rely increasingly on the 
integration of multi-omics data and the development of 
AI technologies. Advances in high-throughput sequencing 
and single-cell sequencing technologies will provide richer 
biological data. However, challenges remain regarding the 
high dimensionality and complexity of data, the difficulty 
of integrating omics data, and clinical translation. Future 
research must continue efforts in algorithm optimization, 
data sharing, and interdisciplinary collaboration to drive 
progress in target identification. Although AIVC tech­
nology holds immense potential, challenges regarding 
data quality, model accuracy, and interpretability persist, 
necessitating systematic analysis to promote high-effi­
ciency and intelligent drug discovery.

features and multimodal representations. Combined with 
big data on toxicology, they support high-throughput 
prediction of multiple endpoints, including acute toxicity, 
carcinogenicity, and organ-specific toxicity.[162] Interpreta­
bility AI methods, such as SHAP and LIME, can identify 
key molecular fragments influencing toxicity, providing a 
quantitative basis for structural optimization. The scala­
bility and self-learning capabilities of AIVC allow them 
to adapt to different toxicity categories and experimental 
scenarios, effectively compensating for the limitations of 
small sample sizes and heterogeneous data processing 
through techniques such as transfer learning.[31]

Challenges and Prospects for Realizing AIVC
Current challenges in AIVC practice
Challenges in multi-source data acquisition, integra­
tion, and computational infrastructure bottlenecks
In the process of constructing high-fidelity AIVC models, 
researchers face comprehensive challenges ranging from 
foundational data acquisition to top-level computational 
architecture.[95] The inherent multi-scale nature of biologi­
cal systems—encompassing molecular interactions, signal 
transduction networks, and dynamic interactions between 
cells and their microenvironments—dictates the extreme 
complexity of their simulation.[109] This complexity is 
primarily reflected in the acquisition and characteriza­
tion of high-quality data. Although high-throughput 
sequencing and advanced imaging technologies, such 
as immunofluorescence staining, are widely applied, 
acquiring comprehensive, dynamic, and high-resolution 
biological data remains time-consuming and prohibitively 
expensive. The generation of experimental data often 
relies on laborious wet-lab procedures, and due to minor 
fluctuations in experimental conditions, the resulting 
datasets are frequently accompanied by significant noise 
and bias. Such “dirty data” or data scarcity issues directly 
constrain the efficacy of downstream model training, 
making it exceptionally difficult to capture nonlinear 
cellular dynamics.[46]
A more intractable issue lies in the integration and stand­
ardization of multi-source heterogeneous data. With the 
advancement of omics technologies, researchers can access 
massive multi-modal datasets, including genomics, tran­
scriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics [Table 1].[177,178] 
However, these data are often generated from different 
experimental platforms, technical batches, or even different 
species backgrounds, exhibiting extremely high heteroge­
neity.[94] For instance, while single-cell sequencing data 
provide high-resolution information at the cellular level, 
its sparsity and high-dimensional characteristics make 
cross-platform integration highly susceptible to “batch 
effects”, causing biological signals to be obscured by 
technical noise.[179] Current modeling tools and algo­
rithms often face challenges in handling high-dimensional, 
nonlinear data characterized by structural missingness, 
thereby failing to achieve efficient data fusion while 
preserving biological consistency. The lack of unified data

As model precision improves and data scale grows explo­

Limitations of current modeling approaches
Although AIVC models have demonstrated huge potential 
to revolutionize life science research and drug discovery 
by simulating biological complexity, their transition from 
proof-of-concept to clinical practice remains obstructed 
by several challenges.
First, the reproducibility and generalizability of AIVC 
models across diverse datasets and experimental plat­
forms represent a significant bottleneck. The inherent 
heterogeneity of biological systems means that cellular 
states and behaviors are dynamically influenced by 
multiple factors, including cell type, genetic background, 
microenvironment, and specific phenotypes. However, 
existing datasets frequently contain substantial techni­
cal variables and experimental noise.[78] For instance, 
high-throughput technologies such as single-cell sequenc­
ing are prone to batch effects. While technical adjustments 
such as “batch effect removal” can mitigate these issues, 
inherent differences in experimental conditions may 
still yield inconsistent sets of differentially expressed 
genes. This data volatility makes it difficult for models 
to capture universal biological structures; instead, they 
tend to overfit patterns specific to a dataset rather than 
identifying genuine causal mechanisms.[110] Many models 
perform excellently during internal validation but suffer 
a sharp decline in performance when applied across 
different experiments or platforms. The root cause lies in 
the instability of perturbation effects within the training 
data and the tendency for weak biological signals to be 
overwhelmed by technical noise. This overfitting to spe­
cific patterns limits the models’ ability to maintain stable 
predictive power in unseen biological contexts (such as 
new cell types or culture conditions), thereby undermin­
ing their reliability in broader applications.[25]
To validate computational predictions via in vitro or 
in vivo experiments makes another major gap in the 
development of AIVC models. Currently, most AIVC 
models remain at the in silico simulation stage, and their 
predictions often lack large-scale, systematic wet-lab vali­
dation.[185] Unlike the static protein structures predicted 
by AlphaFold, cellular behavior is a dynamic evolutionary 
process within an open system, making its prediction far 
more complex than static structural modeling. Significant 
discrepancies exist between ex vivo cell models and real 
physiological environments, and the lack of standardized 
metrics to quantify the differences between virtual organs 
and the actual human microenvironment has created a 
“validation gap”. Furthermore, even advanced founda­
tion models often exhibit unstable zero-shot performance, 
sometimes underperforming simpler baseline models on 
specific tasks.[19] Existing models are deficient in dynamic 
modeling and fail to fully capture the temporal responses 
of cellular behavior. The high cost and long cycles associated 
with biological experiments make translating model pre­
dictions into actionable experimental validation extremely 
challenging. Designing efficient and feasible experimental 
protocols to validate predictions across multiple scales 
and time points remains a critical problem to be solved.[99]
The processing of high-dimensional multi-omics data and 
the potential biases introduced during data integration 
severely compromise model accuracy and interpretability. 
Cell biology data are typically characterized by the “small 
sample size, high feature count” paradigm, leading to 
the “curse of dimensionality”. When integrating mul­
ti-modal data—such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and 
imaging data—cross-scale alignment errors are liable to 
be amplified. This complexity in data integration not 
only increases the computational burden but may also 
introduce systematic biases during the fusion process. Fur­
thermore, distinct genetic perturbations can elicit highly 
similar expression responses, resulting in “information 
collinearity”; if a model fails to distinguish between these, 
it is prone to failure. Concurrently, existing evaluation 
benchmarks may suffer from “over-correction” due to 
an over-reliance on supervised cell-type labels, thereby 
obscuring subtle biological variations within cell types. 
Finally, the inherent “black-box” nature of deep learning 
models limits their interpretability, making it difficult to 
provide clear biological causal explanations.[10,38]

Challenges in the clinical implementation of virtual cell 
models
In the realm of precision medicine, the construction of 
AIVC models capable of accurately predicting cancer 
recurrence, chronic disease progression, and treatment 
optimization represents a central objective. However, despite 
the encouraging prospects demonstrated by existing 
research, it is imperative to recognize that the vast majority 
of current predictive models remain at the in silico stage. 
Their clinical translation is currently impeded by multiple 
barriers, including limited evidence, regulatory deficien­
cies, and ethical complexities.[186]
Limited evidence for clinical validation constitutes the 
primary reality currently facing AIVC predictive models. 
Drawing upon the proposed three-tier milestones for 
AIVC development,[36] we posit that the overall simulation 
maturity of current AIVC technology has only reached 
the first level: predicting and explaining specific biological 
functional responses at a specific time point by integrating 
intrinsic and extrinsic environmental conditions. This 
implies that models are predominantly validated within 
laboratory settings or using in vitro cell line data, while 
their validity and reliability in real-world clinical scenarios 
remain insufficiently substantiated.[187] Furthermore, the 
reliance of most current models on cancer cell lines 
rather than authentic human biology hinders the direct 
application of their insights to drug development, as cell 
lines cannot fully recapitulate the complex physiological 
environment of human tissues.[35] Moreover, while the 
“Virtual Cell Challenge” has undeniably propelled the 
development of perturbation response prediction, its met­
rics primarily assess the goodness-of-fit to in vitro data. 
Unlike AlphaFold, which predicts static structures, pre­
dicting cancer recurrence or chronic disease progression 
involves the long-term dynamic evolution of cells within 
an open system, leading to an exponential increase in 
complexity. Consequently, although models may exhibit 
high precision in silico, their predictive outcomes remain 
clinically unverified hypotheses, lacking support from 
large-scale, multi-center clinical trial data.[188]
Potential barriers to translation extend beyond techno­
logical maturity to deeply involve regulatory and ethical 
considerations. At the regulatory level, there is currently 
a lack of clear approval pathways for the application of 
AI-driven complex biological predictive models in health­
care. AIVC models often possess a “black box” nature, 
with internal decision-making mechanisms that are 
difficult to fully elucidate, posing a challenge to medical 
regulations that demand high transparency and interpreta­
bility. Ensuring model fairness, robustness, and safety 
across diverse patient populations remains a difficult task 
for regulatory bodies. Ethically, constructing a “personal 
virtual cell” to predict individual treatment responses 
involves highly sensitive genomic and pathological data. 
The issue of patient data privacy becomes particularly 
acute when the model application is integrated with clinical 
validation.[10] Furthermore, if personalized treatment 
recommendations provided by the model contradict tra­
ditional clinical experience, or if algorithmic bias leads to 
medical inequity, the attribution of liability—whether to 
the developer, physician, or hospital—remains undefined. 
The absence of comprehensive laws, regulations, and ethi­
cal guidelines makes it difficult for AIVC models to truly 
integrate into mainstream clinical practice.

enhancing their translational relevance. In the context of 
cancer treatment optimization, the OCTO-vc model[35]

Strategies to advance AIVC applications
Advancing AIVC applications hinges on fostering interdis­
ciplinary collaboration. The development and application 
of AIVC span multiple fields, including biology, com­
puter science, and engineering, requiring the integration 
of domain-specific expertise to form comprehensive 
research perspectives. Within biomedicine, collaboration 
between computational biologists and experimental biolo­
gists effectively integrates computational models with 
experimental data, thereby enhancing the accuracy and 
utility of AIVC models. Interdisciplinary partnerships also 
facilitate the development of novel technologies, such as 
utilizing VR and AR for cell model visualization, which 
holds significant importance for education and clinical 
applications.[189]
Open-source sharing of high-quality datasets
Establishing standardized data-sharing platforms represents 
another critical strategy. Data sharing accelerates research 
progress and promotes collaboration and communication 
among different research teams. By creating unified data 
standards and formats, the effective integration and anal­
ysis of data from diverse sources are ensured, improving 
research reproducibility and reliability.[105] Many biomedical 
research platforms have begun adopting the FAIR (Finda­
ble, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable) principles 
to promote data sharing and reuse.[190] Data-sharing 
platforms should also provide user-friendly interfaces 
and tools to lower the technical barriers to data sharing, 
encouraging broader researcher participation in virtual 
cell studies.[191]
Current breakthroughs in AI-powered virtual cell tech­
nology rely on large-scale generation of high-quality 
biomolecular interaction data. A pertinent real-world 
example is the Target 2035 initiative, which utilizes affinity 
selection mass spectrometry (AS-MS) and DNA-encoded 
libraries (DEL) to construct protein-small molecule binding 
databases. DEL enables trillion-scale HTS to capture weak 
interactions, while AS-MS provides medium-throughput, 
high-precision binding kinetics data. This strategy aims to 
cover over 2000 human protein targets within five years, 
generating hundreds of millions of standardized data 
points, including crucial negative datasets to address the 
lack of negative controls in traditional databases.[192]
Such structured databases are revolutionizing virtual cell 
design paradigms. GNNs trained on this data can enhance 
molecular docking accuracy to experimental levels. In 
disease modeling, virtual platforms simulate perturba­
tions in pathological signaling pathways; for instance, 
generative models can rapidly design allosteric inhibitors 
within KRAS mutation models, with predictions aligning 
with experimental validation.[193] Open data ecosystems 
foster algorithm competitions, driving advancements such 
as equivariant GNNs[194] to improve screening efficiency 
for difficult-to-drug targets.
Building predictive AIVC models fundamentally requires 
systematic decoding of dynamic response mechanisms 
to diverse perturbations, which hinges on integrating 
ultra-high-throughput, standardized single-cell resolution 
datasets. Tahoe-100M, currently the largest publicly available 
single-cell perturbation atlas, achieves parallel transcrip­
tomic capture across 50 cancer cell lines—over 100 million 
single cells—in a single experiment through its innovative 
“Cell Village” design. This approach involves applying 
over a thousand drug perturbations in parallel across 
highly diverse cellular models. Key breakthroughs of 
this platform include significant batch effect reduction 
and multi-target coverage of drug mechanisms. Data 
characteristics reveal that clustering in transcriptomic 
space is primarily driven by genetic background rather 
than technical factors, validating platform reproducibility. 
Concurrently, a median coverage of over 1000 cells per 
condition enables the capture of heterogeneous responses 
across subpopulations. Although constructed using can­
cer models, its framework intrinsically supports decoding

Development and application of high-performance 
models
AI-powered virtual cells are rapidly emerging as pivotal com­
putational platforms for deciphering cellular responses 
to genetic and pharmacological perturbations, with 
their application potential in drug discovery and disease 
modeling becoming increasingly prominent.[188] The core 
advantage of virtual cell models lies in their ability to 
systematically simulate transcriptomic dynamics under 
genetic manipulations (e.g., single-gene knockout) or 
drug interventions, thereby substituting extensive wet-lab 
experiments with computational approaches to signifi­
cantly accelerate biological discovery and therapeutic 
strategy development. Exemplified by the recent Virtual 
Cell Challenge (https://virtualcellchallenge.org/), which 
explicitly designates “predicting transcriptional responses 
to single-gene perturbations in novel cellular contexts” as 
its core task, this capability is widely recognized as the 
functional cornerstone of virtual cells. Such generalizable 
predictive capacity is crucial for constructing universal cell 
models, particularly when applied to patient-derived cells. 
Virtual cells can simulate perturbation responses under 
specific genetic backgrounds or disease states, providing 
theoretical guidance for personalized drug screening.[195]
At the model architecture level, Transformer frameworks 
have demonstrated significant superiority in this field due 
to their powerful sequence modeling and context-aware 
capabilities. For instance, the STATE model simultaneously 
integrates population-level variations and single-cell 
heterogeneity through bidirectional attention mecha­
nisms, achieving multi-level modeling of perturbation 
effects.[20] Such architectures capture nonlinear regu­
latory relationships within gene expression networks, 
transcending the limitations of traditional linear models. 
Breakthroughs in model performance fundamentally rely 
on the scale and quality of training data: large-scale 
single-cell perturbation datasets such as Tahoe-100M 
provide foundational resources for learning complex 
dose-response relationships and cell-state transition pat­
terns. When validated under standardized 3D evaluation 
frameworks—incorporating differential gene identifi­
cation, perturbation pattern discrimination, and global 
expression error—the biological relevance and reliability 
of predictions undergo rigorous quantification. This 
represents a milestone for transforming virtual cells from 
research tools into preclinical decision-support systems.[196]
High-profile algorithmic competitions have emerged as a 
pivotal engine driving the iterative advancement of AIVC 
models, with the inaugural AI Virtual Cell Challenge 
held in 2025 serving as a milestone event that marked 
the transition of AIVC construction from theoretical explora­
tion to practical validation. This competition established 
a high-quality benchmark dataset comprising approxi­
mately 300,000 H1 human embryonic stem cells subjected 
to 300 distinct gene perturbations via CRISPRi technology, 
aiming to evaluate the generalization capabilities of models 
on out-of-distribution data rather than mere pattern 
memorization. The results profoundly revealed core strate­
gies for constructing high-performance models: relying 
solely on deep learning architectures often fails to surpass 
statistical baselines, whereas hybrid models integrating 
classical statistical features with neural networks demon­
strate superior performance. For instance, the winning 
solution, xTrimoSCPerturb, effectively overcame the limi­
tations of purely data-driven methods in capturing gene 
sequence relationships and mitigating technical noise by 
explicitly embedding statistical priors, such as differential 
expression gene frequencies, into the scFoundation archi­
tecture and combining them with protein multimodal 
embeddings (https://www.biomap.com/en). Furthermore, 
the application of generative AI techniques, such as Flow 
Matching, provided novel technical pathways for captur­
ing heterogeneous cellular responses to perturbations and 
complex gene interaction details. Nevertheless, despite 
breakthroughs in specific metrics, current virtual cell 
construction faces significant challenges; evaluation data 
indicate a distinct trade-off between different assessment 
dimensions, where optimization for perturbation discrimi­
nation score (PDS) often comes at the expense of mean 
absolute error (MAE), and the vast majority of models 
have yet to surpass basic statistical baselines in absolute 
prediction accuracy at the whole-transcriptome level. This 
suggests that current AIVC models are primarily learning 
relative changes rather than accurately reconstructing 
absolute cellular states, highlighting an urgent need for 
future developments to bridge the gap between metric 
optimization from a machine learning perspective and 
the capture of biological essence from a computational 
biology perspective, thereby enhancing model reliability 
in real-world biological scenarios while improving algo­
rithmic generalization (https://virtualcellchallenge.org/#
winners).

Self-evolution of AIVC models: Transitioning from static 
simulation to dynamic intelligent entities
The self-evolution of AIVC models marks a fundamental 
shift in their role from static simulation tools to dynamic 
intelligent entities capable of active exploration, heralding 
a profound transformation in the paradigm of life science 
research. The core objective of this evolution lies not 
merely in simulating cellular behavior but in constructing 
a self-improving digital twin system. By deepening the 
understanding of biological mechanisms through iterative 
cycles of experimentation and feedback, these systems 
ultimately aim to simulate the essence of life itself, poten­
tially passing a biological Turing test.[48]
The cornerstone of virtual cell evolution is the capacity 
to extract deep knowledge from massive, heterogeneous 
biological datasets. Unlike traditional mechanistic models 
that rely on predefined equations, AI-driven frame­
works, such as AIVC, overcome conventional limitations 
by learning from cross-scale data. For instance, the 
TranscriptFormer model, pre-trained on 112 million sin­
gle-cell data points, demonstrates exceptional cross-species 
generalization and zero-shot learning potential, capable 
of predicting drug responses in unknown species without 
additional training. Similarly, the STATE architecture over­
comes prediction bottlenecks in unobserved cellular 
environments by establishing cross-environment generali­
zation capabilities, reflecting the model’s comprehension 
of biological principles rather than rote memorization. 
Furthermore, the generative framework Squidiff utilizes 
diffusion models to infer continuous dynamic processes 
from sparse data, accurately modeling non-additive gene 
perturbation effects even without prior knowledge, indi­
cating that models are gradually acquiring the complex 
dynamic characteristics of living systems.[15] This para­
digm enables virtual cells to function like LLMs, decoding 
the language of life from biological data, as exemplified 
by CodonFM, which successfully translates RNA codons 
into programmable rules.
The self-evolution of these models relies on a closed-loop 
feedback and autonomous iterative optimization mechanism 
characterized by P-E-D. Within this framework, virtual 
cells are not statically constructed; instead, they formulate 
preliminary theories based on existing data, actively gen­
erate verifiable hypotheses, and design experiments with 
high information gain to challenge their own predictions. 
Feedback from real experimental data prompts the model 
to update its internal knowledge, achieving continuous 
performance improvement. For example, the rbio1 model 
introduces a soft validation mechanism to pre-screen 
hypotheses in a virtual environment, significantly reduc­
ing wet-lab costs,[73] while the DrugReflector framework 
optimizes input features through active learning, substan­
tially improving drug screening hit rates.[34] The CellForge 
framework further embodies this process within a MAS 
capable of autonomously executing the entire workflow—
ranging from literature retrieval and architecture design 
to code generation—thereby realizing the automation and 
intelligence of the scientific research process.[45]
Community-driven open challenges and standardized 
evaluation frameworks have accelerated the self-evolution 
of virtual cells, which are progressively integrating the 
multi-scale complexity of living systems. The AIVC frame­
work fuses data from molecular to tissue levels through a 
hybrid transformer-GNN architecture, while the SToFM 
model extends single-cell patterns to multicellular spatial 
transcriptomics, marking a significant step toward uni­
fied representation.[70] The scButterfly model pioneered a 
cross-modal translation framework, effectively addressing 
the challenge of integrating multi-omics data.[71] Con­
currently, community-driven open challenges, accelerate 
model selection and refinement by establishing bench­
mark datasets and standardized evaluation metrics such 
as Cell-Eval. The zero-shot generalization capabilities 
demonstrated by foundation models such as Tahoe-x1 in 
these benchmarks further validate the effectiveness of 
large-scale pre-training.
Despite these promising prospects, AIVC still faces the 
core challenge of transitioning from static expression 
profiles to dynamic causal networks. Overcoming the 
challenges of dimensionality, data sparsity, and biological 
stochasticity to accurately capture the spatiotemporal 
evolutionary trajectories of cells remains an urgent prob­
lem to be solved. Looking ahead, self-evolving virtual 
cells are poised to give rise to scientist-AI, enabling an 
autonomous closed loop of scientific discovery ranging 
from problem formulation to experimental verification.

The construction of patient-specific virtual cells represents 
a pivotal technology for personalized medicine, achieved 
through the integration of multi-omics data, digital mapping 
of clinical phenotypic data, and continuous monitoring of 
dynamic health states. With advancements in biomedical 
research, the convergence of AI and big data technologies 
shows potential to enable the development of patient-spe­
cific virtual cells, offering novel approaches for disease 
prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. AIVC technology, 
as the core driver in this field, is reshaping the practice of 
personalized medicine.

for constructing patient-specific virtual cells. By integrating 
multi-layered biological information—including genomics,

is essential for personalized medicine. By converting 
clinical features—such as symptoms, signs, and biochemi­

mapping not only enhances the modeling accuracy of 
patient-specific virtual cells but also provides data sup­

can simulate responses to different treatment regimens, 
enabling personalized therapeutic planning.[200] AIVC

construct disease-specific research platforms. Importantly, 
AIVC not only maps relationships between patient out­

cell behavior and microenvironmental changes, serving as 
a HTS system for drug development.[201]

real-time updates to virtual cells. By leveraging wearable 
devices and biosensors, researchers can continuously 
monitor physiological states and promptly adjust AIVC 
models to reflect a patient’s latest health condition. This 
real-time monitoring helps physicians better track disease 
progression and make timely medical adjustments, thereby 
improving treatment outcomes.[202] The AIVC system, 
through periodic updates of each patient’s virtual rep­
resentation, monitors health state evolution, predicts 
adverse events, and assesses potential therapeutic effi­
cacy. Particularly with the integration of novel low-cost 
diagnostic data—such as circulating tumor DNA and 
radiomics from liquid biopsies—AIVC models can be 
continuously refined to generate increasingly personalized 
AIVC models for each patient. Single assays, such as 
transcriptomic or genetic liquid biopsies, reveal multiple 
critical patient characteristics, significantly expanding 
AIVC’s diagnostic potential across a broad spectrum of 
diseases.[10]
Predictive modeling of disease progression
Predictive modeling of disease progression plays an 
increasingly vital role in modern medicine, particularly 
in the management of cancer, chronic diseases, and other 
complex conditions.[203] Accurate predictive models can 
effectively assess patient disease risk, guiding clinical deci­
sion-making and therapeutic strategy formulation.[106] 
AIVC technology brings revolutionary advancements to 
this field through its multi-scale modeling capability and 
capture of intrinsic cellular heterogeneity.
Dynamic assessment of cancer recurrence risk consti­
tutes a critical area within disease predictive modeling. 
Research demonstrates that AI and machine learning 
techniques can effectively analyze clinical data from can­
cer patients to predict recurrence risk.[204] For instance, 
using deep learning algorithms to predict survival rates in 
gastric cancer patients revealed that the model’s accuracy 
remained high across different survival intervals, particu­
larly excelling at timely identification of recurrence risk in 
high-risk patients.[205] Furthermore, AI models can inte­
grate multiple biomarkers and clinical features, enhancing 
recurrence prediction capability and thereby assisting cli­
nicians in developing more personalized follow-up plans 
and treatment strategies.[206] The unique value of AIVC in 
this domain lies in its ability to not only accurately quan­
tify gene expression changes but also simulate functional 
alterations of these genes, such as loss-of-function, PTMs, 
or the rewiring of PPIs and signaling networks, using 
tumor sequencing data. This deep mechanistic modeling 
enables AIVC to incorporate multi-dimensional biologi­
cal information when predicting cancer recurrence risk, 
significantly improving predictive accuracy.[207,208]
Early warning for chronic diseases is another crucial appli­
cation of predictive modeling. The progression of chronic 
conditions like diabetes and cardiovascular disease is often 
insidious, making early identification of deterioration risk 
paramount. By analyzing patient EHRs and biomarkers, 
researchers have developed various AI models capable of 
identifying potential risk factors during the pre-clinical 
stage. For example, utilizing machine learning algorithms, 
researchers successfully predicted the risk of rapid pro­
gression in chronic kidney disease patients, suggesting 
early intervention to improve patient prognosis.[209] AIVC 
demonstrates particular promise in managing chronic 
diseases such as type 1 diabetes.[210] It can model the 
biological phenotype of individual patients, using in silico 
experiments to identify interventions that drive progeni­
tor cell differentiation into β-cells, while simultaneously 
designing methods to protect these cells from immune 
attack and maintain their function. This AIVC-based 
virtual cell engineering may create individualized β-cell 
replacement therapies for type 1 diabetes patients, either 
through transplantation of engineered cells or via in situ 
engineering.[211]

a significant component of disease progression predic­
tive modeling. Cancer patients may experience reduced 
treatment efficacy due to tumor cell drug resistance.[212] 
Recently, leveraging big data analytics and AI, researchers 
have identified biomarkers associated with drug resistance, 
enabling the construction of predictive models. For 
instance, in NSCLC studies, AI algorithms analyzing 
patient genomic data revealed specific gene mutations

Optimization of therapeutic regimens and decision 
support systems
In personalized medicine, the AIVC serves as a robust 
decision support system, providing precise clinical guid­
ance by integrating patient-specific data and simulating 
cellular behaviors. Its core strength lies in multi-scale 
modeling capabilities, which integrate representations 
of biological entities across molecular, cellular, and tis­
sue-level physical scales to capture the holistic landscape 
of disease pathogenesis and progression.[188] This offers 
decision support for therapeutic regimen optimization 
from a systems biology perspective.[100]
AIVC demonstrates significant advantages in optimizing 
cancer chemotherapy regimens. By incorporating molecu­
lar profiles of patient tumor biopsy samples and TME 
characteristics, it constructs personalized AIVC models 
to simulate the tumoricidal efficacy of various chemo­
therapeutic drug combinations and their toxic effects 
on normal tissues. Studies indicate that this AIVC-based 
virtual screening approach has the potential to identify 
personalized drug regimens that maximally inhibit tumor 
growth while minimizing adverse effects.[214]
In rare disease therapeutics, AIVC plays a pivotal role, 
particularly in cell-based therapies. It enables targeted 
modification of pancreatic β-cells, facilitating the develop­
ment of individualized β-cell replacement therapies for 
type 1 diabetes. For immunotherapy, AIVC optimizes chi­
meric antigen receptor-T (CAR-T) cell design and culture 
conditions by modeling CAR-T cell behaviors within the 
TME, thereby advancing novel optimization strategies for 
cancer immunotherapy.[215]
Through the AIVC decision support system, clinicians 
comprehensively evaluate the advantages and limitations 
of different therapeutic options to formulate more precise 
and effective treatment strategies. By periodically updating 
patient-specific virtual instances, AIVC enables continuous 
monitoring of health status evolution, prediction of adverse 
events, and assessment of potential therapeutic outcomes. 
With the integration of novel low-cost diagnostic data 
(e.g., liquid biopsies), AIVC models undergo iterative 
refinement, delivering continuously optimized therapeutic 
recommendations for each patient.[8]
Future advancements in AIVC-based personalized medicine 
are anticipated to enhance treatment success rates, reduce 
healthcare expenditures, and improve patient experiences 
and health outcomes.
Conclusions
AIVC technology is spearheading a paradigm shift in 
biomedical research toward a “simulation-first” approach 
at an unprecedented pace. The profound driving force 
behind this transformation lies in the explosive develop­
ment of high-throughput data generation technologies, 
such as single-cell omics and spatial transcriptomics, 
which provide massive and refined “fuel” for the digital 
modeling of cellular behaviors. Concurrently, the rise 
of advanced AI algorithms—including deep learning, 
generative models, Transformer networks, and GNNs—
has endowed researchers with the capability to learn, 
comprehend, and predict life activities from these com­
plex datasets. The core vision of AIVC is to construct a 
digital twin capable of accurately simulating complex 
biological processes within a computational environment, 
ranging from molecular interactions to the emergence of 
multicellular systems, thereby achieving deep insights into 
the fundamental logic of life.
To overcome the heterogeneity and high-dimensional 
complexity of biomedical data, we propose a comprehensive 
framework based on MAS, aiming to construct high-
fidelity “Virtual Cells” and ultimately realize individualized 
digital twins [Figure 2]. The core of this framework lies in 
utilizing specialized sub-agents to process domain-specific 
modal data, followed by a central coordination mecha­

the MAS first ingests and parses multi-omics data from 
the biological level (e.g., genomics, transcriptomics) 
[Figure 2A] and small-molecule multi-modal data from 
the pharmaceutical chemistry domain (e.g., chemical 
structures, physicochemical properties, and knowledge 
graphs) [Figure 2B]. Subsequently, the data preprocess­
ing layer executes a rigorous standardization pipeline, 
including denoising, batch effect correction, and feature 
engineering, utilizing advanced algorithms like GANs 
to impute high-dimensional sparse data and ensure data 
quality [Figure 2C]. A critical step involves mapping these

Despite the immense potential demonstrated by AIVC, its 
comprehensive implementation faces numerous challenges. 
Foremost are the issues regarding the acquisition, integra­
tion, and standardization of multi-source heterogeneous 
biological data, alongside the consequent bottlenecks in 
computational infrastructure. The inherent complexity 
of biological systems, data sparsity, technical noise, and 
batch effects pose severe tests to model generalization and 
reproducibility. Furthermore, current modeling methodologies 
remain limited; for example, models often lack sufficient 
generalization capabilities across cell types and complex 
perturbation scenarios, and there exists a significant “vali­
dation gap” between in silico simulations and wet-lab or 
clinical applications. Additionally, the “black box” nature 
of deep learning models restricts their interpretability, 
while the absence of robust data privacy protections, ethi­
cal considerations, and regulatory frameworks constitutes 
a practical barrier to the deployment of AIVC in clinical 
practice.
Looking ahead, the development of AIVC will transcend 
simple simulation, advancing toward the construction of 
“self-evolving digital life forms” and ultimately achieving 
a “Turing test” for the essence of life. This requires AIVC 
to evolve into an “AI Scientist” capable of autonomous 
learning, active exploration, hypothesis generation, and 
experimental design. Key directions for development 
include: (1) The construction of unified biological foun­
dation models. Drawing on the success of LLMs, future 
AIVCs will aim to integrate a broader spectrum of biolog­
ical data (including biomechanics, electrophysiology, and 
ethology) to develop URs capable of understanding and 
predicting biological phenomena across species, scales, 
and contexts, effectively becoming a “universal transla­
tor” for all biological information. (2) The realization of 
mechanism-aware AI. Breaking through the “black box” 
limitation, models must not only predict accurately but 
also clearly elucidate the biological mechanisms and causal 
chains behind their predictions. By deeply fusing biological 
prior knowledge with explainable AI technologies, the 
scientific discovery capability and clinical credibility of 
these models will be enhanced. (3) The development of 
real-time, dynamic “digital twins of life”. The ultimate 
goal is to construct dynamic digital twins of individual 
patients, organs, or tissues, continuously updating models 
via real-time data streams from wearable devices and 
liquid biopsies to achieve preventive health management, 
real-time monitoring of disease progression, and dynamic 
adjustment of personalized treatment plans. (4) Actively 
addressing ethical and social challenges. As AIVC capa­
bilities strengthen, ethical and social issues regarding data 
privacy, algorithmic bias, fairness, and accountability 
will become increasingly prominent. Establishing sound 
laws, regulations, ethical guidelines, and regulatory 
frameworks will be critical to ensuring the healthy develop­
ment of AIVC technology and maximizing its societal 
benefits. Through these prospective efforts, AIVC holds 
the promise of fundamentally transforming our ability to 
understand life, intervene in disease, and design biological 
systems, ushering in a new era of precision biology and 
digital medicine.
